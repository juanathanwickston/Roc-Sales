Module 4
Make the Sale
Objectives
By the end of this module, you will be able to:
Understand how different customers define value.
Link product features to customer benefits.
Use a structured process to connect your solution to customer needs.
Use feedback questions to keep the conversation engaging.
Discuss price and respond to objections while reinforcing value.
Customer Focused Presentations
The goal of the Make the Sale stage is to present a solution that addresses the needs and problems discovered during earlier conversations.
Your presentation should be a customer focused discussion, not a one-sided presentation.
A good rule is a 50/50 dialogue between you and the customer.
Keep the customer involved by asking questions, listening carefully, and asking for feedback.
During the presentation:
Repeat the key needs, wants, and problems the customer shared earlier.
Explain how your product or service addresses those needs.
Describe how your solution solves problems and creates value.
Translate product features into meaningful benefits.
Ask for reactions, opinions, and feedback during the discussion.
Features and Benefits
A common saying in sales is:
“Features tell. Benefits sell.”
Salespeople often focus on describing product features. Customers care more about what those features will do for them.
Feature
A feature describes the characteristics of a product or service.
Benefit
A benefit explains what the customer gains from that feature.
A simple way to identify benefits is to ask yourself:
“What does this feature mean to the customer?”
Two helpful phrases when translating features into benefits are:
“What this means to you is…”
“The impact this will have on your business is…”
These statements help customers understand the value behind the feature.
Linking Features and Benefits to Customer Needs
To connect your solution to the customer’s situation, use the following four step process.
Step 1 – Repeat
Restate the need or concern discussed during the interview.
Step 2 – Describe
Explain the product feature that addresses the need.
Step 3 – Convert
Translate the feature into a benefit.
Step 4 – Tell the Outcome
Explain the result the customer or their business will receive.
Example structure:
“You mentioned that ______.” (Need)
“Our product includes ______.” (Feature)
“What this means to you or your organization is ______.” (Feature to benefit conversion)
“This leads to ______.” (Benefit)
This keeps the conversation focused on customer outcomes rather than product details.
Feature to Benefit Exercise
To practice this concept:
Identify several customer needs or problems.
List product or service features that address those needs.
Write how each feature translates into a benefit for the customer or their organization.
Feedback Questions
An effective presentation is not just explaining your product. It also involves asking questions and listening.
Feedback questions serve several purposes:
Maintain a dialogue with the customer.
Help you understand the customer’s opinion about the information you share.
Help you gauge their level of interest.
Allow you to redirect the conversation if needed.
Examples include:
“What are your thoughts on this?”
“Does this relate to the challenges we discussed?”
“How do you see this fitting into your current process?”
Key Ideas for Effective Sales Conversations
Keep these principles in mind during the presentation:
Keep the conversation focused on the needs and problems identified earlier.
Adapt your approach to the customer’s behavioral style.
Remember that customers make decisions using both emotion and logic.
Ask feedback questions to keep the customer involved.
Strengthen the relationship by filling needs, solving problems, and creating value.
Check for agreement throughout the conversation to build acceptance and momentum.
Price, Cost, and Value
Customers often confuse the terms price, cost, and value.
Price
The amount of money paid for a product or service.
Cost
What the buyer pays over time beyond the initial price. This can include time, inconvenience, inefficiencies, or repairs.
Value
The customer’s perception of worth beyond the product itself.
Sometimes a lower price can lead to a higher cost because of inefficiencies or poor performance.
A common saying is:
“Beware the cost of the lowest price.”
Each customer interprets value differently. To present a strong value proposition, you must understand how that specific customer defines value.
Research shows that perceived value is influenced by the overall experience.
A simplified formula for perceived value is:
Customer benefits
plus
Quality of experience
minus
Cost to acquire
equals
Perceived value
Understanding Customer Value
Consider the following question:
What factors might influence your customer’s perception of value?
Understanding these factors helps you position your solution more effectively.
Discussing Price
Customers sometimes focus on price too early in the conversation.
Price discussions are often premature until the customer understands the benefits and agrees that the solution fits their needs.
Financially focused customers are evaluating more than price. They are asking themselves questions such as:
Is this salesperson knowledgeable?
Do they understand my challenges?
Are they credible?
Why should I work with them instead of a lower priced option?
If the discussion focuses only on price or product details, attempts to sell value will often fail.
Before presenting price, ask yourself:
Do I understand what motivates this customer?
Do I understand their business challenges?
Can I speak their language about those challenges?
Can I clearly explain our value proposition?
Can I translate price into value?
If you cannot answer these questions clearly, it will be difficult to present a compelling value proposition.
Suggestions for Presenting Price
When discussing price:
Speak clearly and confidently.
Deliver the price clearly and confidently.
Believe that the value you provide exceeds the cost.
Translate price into value.
Break the price down into daily, weekly, or monthly cost when helpful.
Illustrate return on investment when possible.
Highlight savings or business improvements.
Mention benefits such as convenience, efficiency, pride, or peace of mind.
Responding to Price Objections
When a prospect raises a price objection:
Pause and Listen
Give them space to fully explain their concern.
Explore the Objection
Ask questions to understand the real issue.
Examples:
“What specifically concerns you about the price?”
“Are there budget constraints we should consider?”
“Is there something else holding you back?”
Summarize Their Concern
Repeat their objection in your own words so they know you understand.
Emphasize Value
Shift the conversation back to how your solution solves their problem and creates value.
Offer Alternatives When Appropriate
Discuss options such as different packages, payment terms, or longer commitments if appropriate.
Follow Up
If the prospect remains unsure, follow up later to continue the conversation.
Cushion, Probe, and Respond
A structured approach to handling objections includes four steps.
Cushion
Acknowledge the objection with a neutral statement.
Example:
“Your concern about the investment is important.”
Probe
Ask questions to understand the real issue.
Example:
“How do you usually determine your budget for projects like this?”
Respond
Provide a clear, factual response that addresses the concern.
Example:
“Let me explain how our solution helps you manage costs more effectively.”
Confirm
Check that the customer understands and agrees.
Example:
“Does that make sense?”
After addressing the concern, continue moving the conversation toward a decision.
Do Not Paint Seagulls in the Buyer’s Picture
In sales, the rule “Do not paint seagulls in the buyer’s picture” means presenting only what the buyer expects and values.
The phrase comes from a story about a teacher who added a seagull to a child’s drawing and ruined the child’s vision of the picture.
In sales, this happens when a salesperson introduces unnecessary features or information that the buyer did not ask for.
Avoid overwhelming the buyer. Focus only on the elements that matter to their decision.
Feel, Felt, Found Method
The Feel, Felt, Found technique is a common method for handling objections.
Feel
Acknowledge the customer’s concern.
Example:
“I understand how you feel about the cost.”
Felt
Explain that others have felt the same way.
Example:
“Other customers initially felt the same way.”
Found
Explain what those customers discovered after moving forward.
Example:
“They found that the benefits outweighed the cost once they began using the solution.”
This approach builds rapport, reduces resistance, and helps guide the conversation toward a solution.
Key Teaching Points
Conversations should focus on the customer’s needs, not just product features.
Translate product features into clear business benefits.
Use feedback questions to maintain a two way conversation.
Understand the difference between price, cost, and value.
Respond to objections by listening, probing, and reinforcing value.
Present only the information the buyer needs to make a decision.
